Two bars and a clock

Your health, the base's health, and the number that says how long you lasted. Either bar reaching zero ends the run. This is the assignment that turns a simulation you can watch into a thing you can lose at, and then immediately try again.

Godot 4.3+ 60–90 min 6 steps base game

What the game will have that it doesn't now

  • Two health bars on screen, each fed by a signal rather than polled.
  • Contact damage with invulnerability frames, so walking through a crowd costs one hit rather than instant death.
  • A survival clock — the score, since the run is measured in time.
  • A run that ends and restarts in one keypress, which is what makes it testable at all.

What this deliberately leaves plain

Bars are plain rectangles that jump instantly to their new value. No shake when you're hit, no red vignette, no damage numbers, no bar that drains smoothly toward its target, no sound. All of that is real and all of it is the feel pass — a bar that lerps toward its value is a five-minute change that lands far better once everything else is settled.

What you need

A player that moves, and enemies on an enemies collision layer that can reach it. A Game autoload holding base health if you already built one — step 1 shows the whole file either way, so add the parts you're missing.

Step 1

Everything the run knows about itself

12 min

One autoload holds both bars, the clock, and whether the run is still going. Create game.gd and register it under Project Settings → Globals → Autoload as Game:

Why both bars live in the same object

Because they aren't independent — either one ending the run is a rule about the pair, and a rule about a pair needs somewhere that can see both. Splitting them into a player script and a base script would mean a third thing watching both to decide when to stop, which is more machinery for less clarity.

The running guard is doing real work

Enemies keep arriving for a moment after the base falls, and each of them would call damage_base and re-emit run_ended. One early return stops a game-over screen from firing five times and the final clock reading from being wrong.

Step 2

The HUD

14 min

New scene, root CanvasLayer named HUD. A CanvasLayer draws on top of everything and ignores the world's transform, so the bars stay put.

Children: two ProgressBar nodes named PlayerBar and BaseBar, and a Label named Clock. Put the player's bar top-left, the base's bar bottom-centre near the drain, and the clock top-right. On both bars turn Show Percentage off.

Instance the HUD into the arena scene.

Why the base's bar goes near the drain

Two bars in the same corner become one thing you glance at. Putting the base's health where the base actually is means the bar and the threat share a location — you look down, you see enemies approaching and how much the base can take, in one look. It costs nothing and it's the difference between reading a number and understanding a situation.

Step 3

Getting hurt

15 min

Add an Area2D called Hurtbox as a child of the player, with a CollisionShape2D roughly the player's size. Set its Layer to nothing and its Mask to enemies only.

Call _check_contact(delta) from _physics_process.

Why not body_entered

It fires once, on entry. Stand still inside an enemy and after your invulnerability expires nothing happens — no new entry event, no damage — so the safest place in the game becomes standing inside the thing trying to kill you. Polling the overlap each frame is a line shorter and doesn't have that hole in it.

A full second of invulnerability

It sounds generous. It isn't: enemies arrive in crowds, and without it a single walk through three of them at 60 fps would take your entire health bar in a fraction of a second, with no chance to react. The blink is there so you can see the state — without a visible signal, players read invulnerability as the game failing to register hits.

Step 4

Ending the run

10 min

The autoload already emits run_ended. Something has to listen and stop the world. Add a Label to the HUD named GameOver, centred, hidden by default:

Connect it in _ready with Game.run_ended.connect(_on_run_ended), and hide the label there too.

Then set the HUD node's Process Mode to Always in the inspector, under Node → Process. Everything else can freeze; the thing showing the result cannot.

Pausing stops the autoload's clock too

get_tree().paused = true halts _process on nodes with the default process mode, including Game — which is fine here, because the clock should stop when the run does. Worth knowing it's happening, though: it's the same mechanism that would silently stop a timer you did want running.

Reporting the cause

"Overwhelmed" and "base lost" are different failures and want different responses from you as a player — one says stay safer, the other says stay lower. A game-over screen that only shows a number makes you guess which mistake you made, and guessing is not learning.

Step 5

Try again in one key

8 min

Add a restart action bound to R in the input map, then:

Unpause before you reload, and reset before that

Reload while paused and the fresh scene arrives frozen. Reload without resetting and the autoload keeps the dead run's health and clock, so the new run starts at zero health and ends instantly. Both are one-line mistakes that look like much deeper bugs.

Why one key matters more than it sounds

You are about to play this game thirty times in a row to tune it. Every second between dying and being back in is a second of that tuning you won't do. A game you can restart without thinking is a game you'll actually iterate on — this is a tooling decision disguised as a gameplay one.

Step 6

Play ten runs

15 min

This is the assignment, and it's the first time the whole game exists. Play ten full runs and write down the time and cause of each. Ten, not two — the pattern in the causes is the finding, and you can't see a pattern in two.

What the ten runs showWhat it meansWhere to look
Nearly all "base lost"You can survive but can't defend — interception is too slowArena gaps, or the movement values
Nearly all "overwhelmed"Being where you need to be is fatalPlayer health, invulnerability time, walker speed
A mix, and it variesBoth bars are live threats — this is the targetNothing. Note the numbers and stop.
Times clustered within a few secondsThe ramp dominates and skill barely registersLower ramp_per_spawn
Times all over the placeSomething random is deciding the run, not youSpawn positions, or a gap that funnels everything

Then push two values to see what they own. Set invuln_time to 0.2 and play once — the crowd becomes lethal and you'll stop defending the bottom floor at all. Set it to 3.0 and you can walk through anything, which makes the player bar decorative. Somewhere in between, contact damage is a real cost that you're sometimes willing to pay. That willingness is the decision the whole game is made of.

The question this batch was really asking

Is the tension between the two bars actually interesting? You should feel yourself choosing — go down and risk getting hit, or stay safe and let the base take one. If you never feel that choice, the fix is usually the arena rather than the numbers: the distance between where it's safe and where you're needed is what creates the dilemma.

Write down all ten times and causes. That list is the baseline every feel and balance assignment measures against — and it's the difference between "the game feels better now" and "runs got twelve seconds longer".

That's the assignment

The base game is done. It has a threat, a verb, two ways to lose, a score, and a restart — which means from here on you're improving a game rather than building one. That's a much better place to be making decisions from, and it's why this batch was worth doing plainly.
