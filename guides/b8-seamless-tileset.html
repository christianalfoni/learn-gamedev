Sketch B8 · Aseprite

A seamless tileset

One texture that tiles without a visible seam, then the edge pieces that let it end. Drawn in Aseprite's tilemap mode, which was built for exactly this and which most people never turn on. No engine in this sketch — just the art, done properly.

Aseprite 1.3+ 90–120 min 8 steps 16 × 16 tiles

What you'll have at the end

  • A seamless 16 × 16 base texture you verified by tiling it, not by hoping.
  • A 13-tile edge set — the smallest set that can enclose any blob shape — plus two variants to break up repetition.
  • A working understanding of Aseprite's tilemap layers: draw once, and every instance of that tile updates everywhere.
  • A PNG export laid out on a regular grid, ready for any engine's tileset importer.

How this guide is built

The drawing is taught by video; the structure is taught here. Prep video 01 is a working pixel artist using Aseprite's tileset features for half an hour — it's the best demonstration of the tooling that exists. These steps give you the tile-count logic, the seam test and the export discipline that the video doesn't spell out.

Step 1

Grid, palette, and one material

8 min

New file, 256 × 256 — room for a grid of tiles with space to experiment. Then:

  • View → Grid → Grid Settings: 16 × 16, and turn the grid on. Every decision from here is relative to that cell.
  • Five colours. A three-step ramp for the material plus two accents. Tilesets show their palette far more than characters do, because there's so much more of them on screen.
  • One material. Grass, stone, dirt or sand — pick the one you find least interesting. Interesting materials tempt you into detail, and detail is what breaks tiling.

Why 16 × 16 rather than 32

Tile size sets the resolution of your entire level geometry — a 32-pixel tile means the smallest ledge you can build is 32 pixels. 16 is the classic compromise: fine enough to build interesting shapes, coarse enough that a full set is an evening's work rather than a week's.

Step 2

The seamless base, using tiled mode

18 min

This is the tile that fills the middle of everything, so it has to survive being repeated hundreds of times. Draw it in one 16 × 16 cell, then turn on View → Tiled Mode → Tiled in Both Axes.

Tiled mode is the whole trick: the canvas now repeats as you draw, so you're editing the texture and previewing the repetition at the same time. A stroke that runs off the right edge appears on the left.

Two rules that decide whether a texture tiles well:

  • Nothing lands in the exact centre of the cell, and nothing sits perfectly in a corner. Both create a grid pattern the eye locks onto immediately.
  • No feature large enough to recognise. If there's one distinctive rock, you'll see that rock forty times. Keep the texture below the threshold where any element becomes memorable.

The test that matters

Zoom out until you can see roughly eight by eight repetitions at 100%. Now look for a pattern — a diagonal line of similar pixels, a repeated bright spot, a visible grid. Your eye will find it in about two seconds if it's there, and once seen it can't be unseen. Fix it by moving the offending detail off-centre, or by dimming it.

Step 3

Turn the layer into a tilemap layer

12 min

Here's the feature almost nobody turns on. Layer → New → New Tilemap Layer, with the grid set to 16 × 16. From now on every cell you draw into is a reference to a tile in a tileset, not loose pixels.

What that buys you is the reason to bother: edit a tile once and every placement of it updates. Change the grass texture in one cell and all forty cells using it change with it. For a tileset — where the entire point is reuse — that turns a tedious asset into something you can iterate on.

Copy your base texture into the tilemap layer, and you'll see it register as tile 1 in the tileset panel.

Tilemap layers and normal layers are different things

You can't paint freehand across cell boundaries on a tilemap layer the way you can on a normal one — every stroke is confined to the tile you're editing. That's the point, but it's disorienting for the first ten minutes. If you want a sketch layer to draw over the whole canvas, keep it as a separate normal layer above.

Step 4

Work out how many tiles you actually need

10 min

Before drawing thirty tiles, count. For a material that needs to end — grass with edges where it meets air — the tile you need depends on which sides are occupied by the same material. Four sides, each either filled or empty, gives sixteen combinations.

So the shopping list is: 1 fill + 4 sides + 4 outer corners + 4 inner corners = 13. Draw them in that order — fill first, then sides, then corners — because each one is derived from the previous.

Why inner corners are the ones people forget

An outer corner is where the material ends and turns — the corner of a platform. An inner corner is where two runs of material meet in an L, and the join needs its own tile or you get a visible notch. It's the most common hole in a homemade tileset, and you only notice when you paint a shape that needs it.

Step 5

The four sides

20 min

Start with the top edge — the one that gets seen most, because it's what the player walks on. Take a copy of the fill tile and give the top few pixels the treatment that says "this is where the material stops": lighter grass blades, a lip of brighter stone, a darker soil line beneath.

Then left, right and bottom. Left and right are usually mirrors of each other, and Aseprite will flip a selection for you — but check the light direction afterwards. If your light comes from upper-left, a mirrored right edge has its highlight on the wrong side, and that reads as wrong even when nobody can say why.

Edges must still tile with themselves

A run of ten top-edge tiles sits side by side, so the top tile has to be seamless left-to-right just like the fill was. Turn tiled mode back on and check a row of them. The most common failure is a grass blade that only works in isolation and produces an obvious repeating comb when placed in a row.

Where the time goes

The top edge takes half of this step and the other three take the rest. That's correct — the top edge carries the visual identity of the whole material. Bottom edges are frequently barely visible and can be much simpler than they feel like they should be.

Step 6

Corners, outer then inner

15 min

Each outer corner is the intersection of two edge tiles you've already drawn. Copy the top edge, copy the left edge, and reconcile them where they meet — that reconciliation is the whole tile. Do the top-left, then mirror and adjust for the other three.

Inner corners are the opposite: mostly fill, with the edge treatment appearing only in one corner where two runs meet. They're quicker than they look once you've done one.

Consistency beats correctness here

Nobody can tell whether a 16-pixel corner is anatomically right. What they can tell instantly is whether the corner uses the same lip thickness and highlight colour as the edges either side of it. Copy from the neighbouring tiles rather than drawing fresh, and the set stays coherent by construction.

Step 7

Two variants, and the export

12 min

Duplicate the fill tile twice and change each one slightly — a couple of pebbles, a patch of darker grass, a crack. Not a feature; a difference. These get sprinkled among the plain fill to break the eye's pattern-matching.

Then export. File → Export, PNG, at 100% scale with no border padding. Lay the tiles out on a regular grid — fill and variants on one row, sides on the next, corners on the third — because every engine's tileset importer wants a uniform grid and a tidy one makes the later setup obvious rather than a puzzle.

No padding, no scaling, nothing clever

Exporting at 2× or adding extra spacing between tiles will make an engine's grid settings not line up, and the symptom — one-pixel slivers of the neighbouring tile appearing along edges — looks like a rendering bug rather than an export mistake. Keep the source at 1×, and scale in the engine if you need to.

Step 8

Build something with it

15 min

This is the sketch. A tileset is only as good as the shapes it can make, and the only way to find the holes is to paint with it.

On the tilemap layer, build a small level fragment by hand — no engine, just placing tiles. Include all of these deliberately, because each one exercises a different tile:

  • A long flat platform (fill + top edge + two outer corners)
  • A one-tile-wide pillar (needs both side edges on the same tile — do you have it?)
  • An L-shaped ledge (inner corner)
  • A floating island, enclosed on all four sides
  • A large solid mass, at least 6 × 6, to check the fill and variants at scale

What you're looking for

Two failure modes, and both are normal on a first set. Missing tiles — a shape you can't build without an ugly compromise, usually the one-tile-wide pillar or a diagonal. Visible repetition — the six-by-six mass showing a pattern that the single tile never revealed. Fix the first by drawing the missing tile; fix the second by adjusting the fill, which now updates everywhere at once because you're on a tilemap layer.

Write down which tile you found missing. It'll be the same category next time — most people have a consistent blind spot, and knowing yours is worth more than the tileset.

That's the sketch

You've got a tileset that can enclose any shape, and the more valuable thing: the habit of counting the tiles before drawing them, and of testing a texture by repeating it rather than by admiring it alone. The tileset is disposable; the seam test isn't.
