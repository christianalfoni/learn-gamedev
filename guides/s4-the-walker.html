The walker

The only enemy in the game. It walks, it turns when it hits a wall, and it falls off every ledge it meets. It has no idea you exist. Most of this assignment is about what you deliberately don't write.

Godot 4.3+ 45–75 min 6 steps base game

What the game will have that it doesn't now

  • An enemy that descends the arena on its own, which is the pressure the entire game is built around.
  • Turning at walls, falling off ledges, and no pathfinding of any kind.
  • Health and death via the take_damage() convention, so the gun works on it with no changes.
  • A measured descent time for a real walker, which is the number the spawn rate gets balanced against.

What this deliberately leaves plain

No animation, no death effect, no hit flash, no particles, no sound, no knockback. An enemy currently vanishes the instant its health runs out, which will feel abrupt and unsatisfying — correctly so. Making a kill feel good is the impact-feel assignment, and it wants a real enemy to work on.

What you need

An arena with walls on the walls layer and one-way platforms on floors. Two StaticBody2D platforms will do if you're testing this on its own — you just need something to walk along and something to fall off.

Step 1

The enemy scene

10 min
  1. New Scene → Other Node → CharacterBody2D, rename it Walker.
  2. A CollisionShape2D with a RectangleShape2D about 12 × 14.
  3. A ColorRect to match, in a colour you'd never mistake for the player.
  4. Collision → Layer: enemies only. Mask: walls and floors only.
  5. Save as walker.tscn.

Why the walker doesn't mask the player

If it did, the two would push each other around — you'd shove enemies down the level, or get pinned against a wall by a crowd. Contact damage doesn't need physical collision: the player detects overlap with an Area2D instead, which is the health-bar assignment's job. So the walker never collides with the player, never notices the player, and never needs a single line about the player.

Make it visually distinct now

In a minute there will be a dozen of these on screen moving in different directions. If the walker and the player are both plain rectangles in similar colours, every test after this becomes harder than it needs to be for the sake of not picking a colour.

Step 2

Walk, and be subject to gravity

12 min

Attach a script to the root:

Drop one into the arena on a middle floor and run it. It should walk in a straight line at a steady pace, fall when the floor runs out, and land on whatever is below.

Constant speed, no acceleration

The player gets acceleration eventually, because the player is being driven and wants weight. The walker is being read — you need to judge, at a glance, where it will be in two seconds so you can decide whether to go and meet it. Constant velocity makes that prediction exact, and predictability is what makes routing a skill rather than a guess.

40 pixels per second is slow, deliberately

About two and a half seconds to cross a 100-pixel floor. It should look almost leisurely on its own — the threat in this game comes from how many there are, not from any one of them being fast. Try 120 to see the difference: a single fast walker is startling, and a crowd of them is unmanageable rather than difficult.

Step 3

Turn at walls

10 min

The entire AI, in two lines. Add to the end of _physics_process, after move_and_slide():

Order matters here in a way that fails quietly

Check is_on_wall() before the move and it reports last frame's state — the walker embeds itself a pixel into the wall before turning, or turns a frame early and never quite touches. Neither crashes, both look subtly wrong, and you'd waste twenty minutes on the wall shapes before suspecting the line order.

Watch two of them meet

Put two walkers on the same floor heading toward each other. They pass straight through — they're not on each other's collision mask. That's a decision, not a bug: enemies that block each other would form queues and pile up against walls, and a crowd that occupies the same space is far easier to read at a glance than one that jostles.

Step 4

Fall off ledges — by writing nothing

8 min

This step has no code. That's the point of it.

Nearly every patrolling-enemy tutorial you'll find adds a downward RayCast2D at the front of the enemy, and turns it around when the ray stops finding ground. It's the standard solution, it's two nodes and three lines, and it is exactly wrong for this game.

An enemy that turns at ledges never leaves the floor it spawned on, never reaches the drain, and never threatens the base. The walker descends because it's too stupid to notice a drop — and that stupidity is what makes gap placement in the arena a difficulty control rather than decoration.

Worth knowing you skipped it deliberately

The reason to spend a step on writing nothing is that in three weeks you'll be reading this code and thinking "the enemy is missing a ledge check". Leaving a comment there — walks off ledges on purpose, this is the descent mechanic — is worth more than the code it replaces.

Step 5

Health and death

10 min

The gun looks for a take_damage method and doesn't care about anything else, so implementing it is all that's needed:

Why two hits rather than one

One-hit kills make the fire rate the only thing that matters — you sweep the button and everything in the line dies. Two makes you commit to a target for a moment, which is what gives a crowd its weight: you can't clear six enemies as fast as you can clear three. It's also the value most worth playing with once there's a real crowd, so keep it exported.

queue_free, not free

The walker is being deleted from inside a collision callback, in the middle of the physics step. queue_free() waits for the end of the frame; free() does it immediately and can crash the engine mid-iteration. The habit costs nothing and the failure is ugly.

Step 6

Let a crowd loose

14 min

This is the assignment. One walker tells you the code runs; eight tell you whether the game works.

Place eight walkers by hand across the top two floors, some facing left, some right. Run it and don't touch the controls — just watch for thirty seconds.

Watch forWhat it tells you
Time from the top floor to the drainThe real descent number. Compare it with the falling-box estimate from the arena assignment — walkers are usually much slower, because they have to find a gap.
Where they bunch upFloors with no gap become holding pens. That's a feature, but you should know where yours are.
A walker that never descendsA floor whose gaps it can't reach, or a pocket. Either the arena needs a gap moved, or you've found a genuine trap.
Walkers vibrating in placeA space one tile wide — it hits both walls each frame and flips forever.
Whether you can read the crowd at a glanceThe single most important thing here. If you can't tell where they'll be in two seconds, you can't route.

Then take the controls and try to kill all eight before any reaches the bottom. You'll fail, because there's no spawner yet and no base to protect — but the attempt is the first time this is a game rather than a collection of systems, and it will tell you more about your arena than any amount of looking at it will.

The thing you're actually testing

Whether the pressure is legible. Eight slow, dumb walkers should feel like a manageable problem you have to move fast to solve — not a swarm you can't parse and not a queue you can stand still and mow down. If it's the first, widen the gaps or slow them; if it's the second, your floors probably need fewer gaps so they can't all funnel down one route.

Write down the descent time and the walk speed you settled on. The spawner assignment balances directly against those two numbers — spawn rate only means something relative to how long a walker survives on the way down.

That's the assignment

The game now has its threat, and it's three dozen lines of code with no intelligence in it at all. Everything interesting about the pressure comes from the arena's shape and the number of them — which means you tune this game by moving tiles and changing one integer, not by writing AI.
