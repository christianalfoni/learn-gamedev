The spawner and the drain

Enemies arrive at the top on a clock that speeds up, and anything reaching the bottom takes a bite out of the base and disappears. This is the assignment where the collection of systems starts behaving like a game that's happening to you.

Godot 4.3+ 60–90 min 6 steps base game

What the game will have that it doesn't now

  • A spawner dropping enemies in at the top, on an interval that shrinks as the run goes on.
  • A drain at the bottom that removes escapees and damages the base.
  • A Game autoload holding base health, so nothing has to go looking for anyone else to report to.
  • A run that runs without you — you can put the controller down and watch the base die, which is the first real measure of whether the difficulty is anywhere near right.

What this deliberately leaves plain

No spawn animation, no warning telegraph, no sound when the base is hit, no screen flash. An enemy reaching the bottom is currently a number changing in a corner — which is exactly the sort of thing the feel passes exist to fix, and they can't fix it until it exists.

What you need

An arena with floors and gaps, and a walker scene — a CharacterBody2D with a direction variable that walks, turns at walls and falls off ledges. If you don't have one, any body that moves downward through the level will do to test the plumbing; even a RigidBody2D box on the enemies collision layer works for steps 4 to 6.

Step 1

One place that knows the base's health

10 min

The drain needs to report damage, and later the HUD needs to display it, and neither should have to find the other in the scene tree. In Godot that's an autoload.

Create game.gd in the project root:

Register it: Project Settings → Globals → Autoload, path res://game.gd, node name Game. It's now reachable as Game from any script in the project.

Why a signal as well as a variable

Anything that wants to know can read Game.base_health. But a health bar shouldn't poll a number sixty times a second on the chance it changed — it should be told. Emitting a signal on every change means the HUD updates exactly when there's something to update, and the drain never needs to know a HUD exists.

Guard the already-dead case

Without the early return, three enemies arriving after the base has already fallen each emit base_destroyed, and whatever ends the run gets told to end it three times. That's the sort of thing that surfaces much later as a game-over screen appearing twice.

Step 2

The spawner

14 min

In the arena scene, add a Node2D named Spawner with three children:

  • Two Marker2D nodes near the top, one toward each side — where enemies appear.
  • A Timer with One Shot on. The script restarts it each time, which makes the changing interval obvious rather than hidden in a property.

Assign walker.tscn to the spawner's Walker Scene slot in the inspector, then run it. Enemies should start appearing and making their way down.

Random direction matters more than it sounds

Spawn every walker facing the same way and they all take the same route down, arriving in a neat procession. Randomising it means the crowd spreads across the floor and finds different gaps, which is the difference between a queue and a situation.

Step 3

Rising pressure

10 min

The score is time survived, so the run has to get harder on its own — otherwise a competent player survives forever and the number stops meaning anything.

Then, at the end of _spawn(), shave the interval before restarting the timer:

Per spawn, not per second

Ramping on a clock means the pressure rises whether or not the player is coping. Ramping per spawn ties difficulty to how much has actually come at you — and because faster spawning also makes the ramp faster, it accelerates naturally without a curve to tune. It's one subtraction and it behaves like something much more considered.

Never let the interval reach zero

Without min_interval, a long run eventually asks the timer to wait no time at all, and you get an unbounded spawn loop that locks the frame. The floor is the difference between "hard" and "the game stops responding".

Step 4

The drain

14 min

Make the arena's lowest floor solid — a wall tile, not a one-way platform — so nothing falls out of the world. Then add an Area2D named Drain with a CollisionShape2D spanning the full width of the arena, sitting on that lowest floor, about one enemy tall.

Set the Drain's Collision → Layer to nothing at all, and its Mask to enemies only.

Why the mask is the guard

There's no type check in that handler and there doesn't need to be one. The player is on a different layer and simply never triggers this area — which is also why the player can stand on the bottom floor to intercept without setting off the alarm. Encoding the rule in the mask rather than in an if means it holds for everything you add later, for free.

If you'd rather enemies fall out of an open bottom

That works too — leave the bottom of the arena open and place the Drain below it. But then the player can fall out as well, so you'll need a solid catch floor underneath the drain, and a decision about what falling out means for them. The solid-bottom version avoids the question entirely, and has the side benefit that you can see enemies arrive rather than watching them vanish off the edge.

Step 5

A readout, so you can see it happening

8 min

Base health is currently a number in memory with no way to observe it. Two minutes of scaffolding fixes that — a proper HUD is its own assignment, but you can't test this one blind.

Add a CanvasLayer to the arena with a Label child, and attach:

Call it once on ready

Signals only fire on change, so a label that only listens starts out blank until the first enemy gets through — which looks exactly like a broken connection. Calling the handler directly in _ready gives it a correct starting value, and it's a habit worth having for every signal-driven display.

Step 6

Put the controller down

14 min

This is the assignment. Run it and don't play. Let the spawner run, let the enemies descend, and time how long the base survives with nobody defending it.

That number is the most useful thing you'll get out of this whole batch. Call it the undefended time:

Undefended timeWhat it meansWhat to change
under 30 sToo brutal — a good player barely changes the outcomeRaise start_interval, or offset the arena's gaps more
45–90 sAbout right. Skill has room to matter, and the ramp still ends itNothing yet
over 3 minThe ramp is too gentle; runs will overstay their welcomeRaise ramp_per_spawn, or lower min_interval
never diesmin_interval is above your walker throughput, or enemies are stuckCheck for a floor with no reachable gap

Then play it properly and time it again. The gap between the two numbers is how much of this game is you — if defending only buys you fifteen seconds, the arena is beating the player rather than challenging them.

The moment worth waiting for

Somewhere around the point where the interval halves, the run stops being a queue of individual enemies and becomes a flow you have to manage. That transition is the game arriving. If it never happens, the ramp is too slow; if it happens in the first fifteen seconds, it's too fast and there's no calm to lose.

Write down the undefended time and the defended time. Every difficulty conversation from here — and there will be several — starts with those two numbers.

That's the assignment

The game now runs on its own and can be lost. That's a real threshold: everything before this was systems, and everything after it is tuning and feel. You can also, for the first time, answer "is this any good?" by playing rather than by imagining.
