Sketch B6 · Module B — Aseprite, ending in Godot

The three-state character

One small character with three animations — idle, run, jump — drawn with tags in Aseprite, imported into Godot with Aseprite Wizard, and switched from code. The whole pipeline end to end, at a scale you can finish in one sitting.

Aseprite 1.3+ Godot 4.5+ 90–120 min 9 steps

What you'll have at the end

  • A single .aseprite file holding three tagged animations — 11 frames in total, which is fewer than it sounds.
  • Those tags arriving in Godot as SpriteFrames animations with the right timing, without you ever exporting a sprite sheet by hand.
  • A character that picks its own animation from how it's moving, and faces the way it's going.
  • A save-and-see loop: change a frame in Aseprite, re-import, and watch it update in Godot.

How this guide is built

The drawing itself is taught by video here, not by me. I can give you the constraints, the frame counts, the tag discipline, the whole Godot side and a way to check your own work — but for "how do I actually move these pixels", a person drawing on screen beats any amount of prose. The two prep videos below are doing that job, and the steps are built around them.

Step 1

Set the constraints before you draw anything

8 min

Pixel art punishes indecision late and rewards it early. Decide all of this now, write it on a sticky note, and don't renegotiate mid-sketch:

  • Canvas 32 × 32. Big enough for a readable character, small enough that a walk cycle is an evening rather than a week.
  • Eight colours, maximum. Build the palette first. Two skin, two shirt, two trousers, one outline, one accent is a complete character.
  • Four frames per loop, three for the jump. Eleven frames total.
  • One character, facing right. You'll get left for free in step 8.

In Aseprite: File → New at 32 × 32. Then View → Grid → Grid Settings to 1 × 1, turn on Pixel-Perfect in the pencil options, and open Window → Timeline if it isn't already showing. Turn on onion skinning in the timeline — the ghosted neighbouring frames are the single most useful thing on screen once you're animating.

Why four frames and not twelve

Low frame counts read as snappy rather than cheap, and they're the reason pixel-art characters feel responsive. A four-frame run at ~10 fps is a completely standard game-feel choice, not a compromise. More frames mostly buys you smoothness you didn't want and an evening you don't get back.

Step 2

The base pose, silhouette first

15 min

One frame. A character standing, facing right. Draw it as a solid black silhouette before you add a single colour — if the shape doesn't read as a person in solid black, no amount of shading will save it.

Then, on the same frame: outline, flat colours, and one pass of shading. Keep the head slightly oversized; it reads better at this scale and gives you something to bob in step 3.

The check that matters

Zoom to 100% — actual size, not the comfortable 800% you've been drawing at. This is the only size that tells the truth. If you can't tell where the arms are, the design is too busy for 32 pixels. Simplify now, before you multiply it by eleven frames.

Put the character on its own layer

Add a background layer underneath in a mid grey so you can see your outline, and keep the character on a separate transparent layer. In step 6 you'll exclude that background from the export, and that's only possible if it was never merged in.

Step 3

Idle — four frames of almost nothing

12 min

Copy your base pose across four frames. Then change almost nothing:

  1. Frame 1 — the base pose, untouched.
  2. Frame 2 — the whole body down one pixel. Not the feet.
  3. Frame 3 — down two pixels, and let the head lag one pixel behind the body.
  4. Frame 4 — same as frame 2, on the way back up.

That's a breath. It's one or two pixels of movement and it's the difference between a character and a sticker. Set the frame duration to around 150 ms — idles are slow.

Why the head lags

Offsetting one part by a frame is the cheapest possible version of follow-through: parts of a body don't all arrive at the same time. At 32 pixels you can't animate cloth or hair properly, so a one-pixel, one-frame delay on the head is doing the entire job of selling weight.

Step 4

Run — the four-frame cycle

20 min

This is the hard one, and it's where prep video 01 does the teaching. The four poses it names are the classic cycle:

Two things worth knowing before you start, because they save the most time:

  • Frame 3 is frame 1 with the legs swapped. Copy it and redraw the legs rather than starting fresh. Same for 4 versus 2.
  • The body rises and falls. Frame 2 is the lowest point, frame 4 the highest. A run with a level head is the classic "walking on ice" look.

Frame duration around 100 ms — runs are roughly half the frame time of an idle. Tag it in step 6; for now just get the four frames drawn.

Scrub it constantly

Hit play in the timeline every couple of edits rather than drawing all four and then looking. Animation errors are invisible while still and obvious in motion, and the fastest way to fix a bad cycle is to catch it two pixels in.

Step 5

Jump — three frames, and it doesn't loop

10 min

The jump is the easy one, because it isn't a cycle. Three frames, played once:

  1. Crouch — knees bent, body compressed by two or three pixels. This is anticipation, and it's the frame people skip.
  2. Rise — body stretched, arms up, legs tucked slightly.
  3. Fall — arms out, legs reaching down, ready to land.

In step 8 the code holds the jump animation for the whole time the character is airborne, so frame 3 is what's on screen for most of a fall. Make it the one that reads best.

Squash and stretch, three frames deep

The crouch is squash, the rise is stretch. It's the same principle sketch A4 does with a damped spring on a sprite's scale, except here you're drawing it by hand — which is a better way to understand what the spring is imitating.

Step 6

Tags, timing and layers — the contract with Godot

8 min

Everything so far was drawing. This step is the part that makes the drawing usable, and it's where most Aseprite-to-engine pipelines go wrong.

Select each animation's frame range in the timeline, right-click, New Tag, and name them exactly:

  • idle — frames 1–4, loop
  • run — frames 5–8, loop
  • jump — frames 9–11, no loop

Then set frame durations. Aseprite works in milliseconds and the Wizard converts them to Godot's FPS for you, so tune the timing here where you can see it, not in the engine. Select a tag's frames, right-click, Frame Properties, and set the duration.

Finally, layers: your grey background must not end up in the export. Either delete it, or hide it and rename it something like _bg so you can exclude it by pattern in the Wizard's settings — that's the approach in prep video 02.

Tag names are case-sensitive across the whole pipeline

A tag called Idle becomes an animation called Idle, and sprite.play("idle") in step 8 will quietly do nothing. Lowercase everything now and it stays a non-issue forever.

Step 7

Into Godot with Aseprite Wizard

15 min

Prep video 02 covers this from about 2:00 onward. In short:

  1. Install Aseprite Wizard from the in-editor Asset Library, then enable it in Project Settings → Plugins.
  2. Tell it where Aseprite lives: Editor Settings → Aseprite → set the executable path. On macOS that's inside the .app bundle; on Windows it's the Aseprite.exe in the install folder. Nothing works until this is right, and the error message if it's wrong is not especially loud.
  3. Save your .aseprite file into the project — a sprites/ folder is fine.
  4. Add an AnimatedSprite2D to your player scene, select it, and use the Wizard's inspector dock to import the file as SpriteFrames. Your three tags arrive as three animations with the timing you set in Aseprite.

Set Texture Filter to Nearest on the AnimatedSprite2D (or project-wide in Project Settings → Rendering → Textures → Canvas Textures → Default Texture Filter). Without it your pixel art is blurry and every subsequent judgement you make about it is wrong.

The loop that makes this worth it

Change one pixel in Aseprite, save, re-import in the Wizard dock, and watch it change in Godot. That round trip — seconds, not minutes, and no sprite sheet in sight — is the actual payoff of this sketch. Do it once deliberately just to feel it.

Step 8

Let the movement choose the animation

12 min

The character should never be told which animation to play. It should be asked how it's moving, and answer with an animation. That's four lines:

Call _animate() from _physics_process, just before move_and_slide(). If you've done sketch A1, paste that movement code in and you're finished — otherwise the minimum that makes this testable:

Use play(), never assign animation

Calling sprite.play("run") every frame is safe — the docs are explicit that calling it with the animation already playing resumes rather than restarts. But assigning sprite.animation = "run" every frame resets the frame counter and frame_progress, so your run animation freezes on frame one and you spend twenty minutes blaming Aseprite.

The 8.0 threshold

Comparing against a small number rather than zero means the run animation stops while the character is still sliding the last few pixels to a halt. Compare against exactly 0 and you get a frame or two of running-in-place at the end of every stop.

Step 9

The read test

12 min

This is the sketch. Everything before it was preparation for looking properly.

Run the game and work through these deliberately. Each one is a question you can answer by looking, which is why they work without anyone reviewing your art:

TestHowWhat a failure looks like
Actual sizeView the game at 1× with no zoomYou can't tell which way the character faces
SilhouetteSet modulate to black in the inspector while it runsIdle and run are indistinguishable as shapes
TransitionRun, then stop, then jump from standing, then jump while runningA visible pop, or a frame of the wrong animation
FlipRun left, stop, run rightThe character faces right the moment it stops
TimingHalve then double every duration in Aseprite, re-importYou can't tell which version is better — look again
Loop seamWatch the run for 20 seconds without blinkingA hitch where frame 4 meets frame 1

Then break it on purpose, because that's where the learning is. Set the idle to 40 ms — a character having a panic attack. Set the run to 400 ms — underwater. Delete the crouch frame from the jump and see how much anticipation was doing. Put them back when you're done, or don't.

The one that surprises people

The silhouette test. Colour hides an enormous amount of ambiguity, and a character that reads beautifully in full colour can be completely illegible in black. If idle and run look the same in silhouette, the run needs more extreme leg positions — not more shading.

Write down what you changed in the tuning pass. Frame durations especially — next time you draw a cycle you'll start from numbers you already trust instead of guessing again.

That's the sketch

You've drawn eleven frames and, more usefully, you now own the whole path from a pixel in Aseprite to a frame on screen in Godot — the part that stops most people from using their own art. The character is disposable. The pipeline and the read test aren't.
