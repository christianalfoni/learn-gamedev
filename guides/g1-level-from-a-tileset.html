A level from a tileset

A tileset PNG goes in; a level you can walk around comes out. Autotiling that picks the right edge pieces as you paint, collision that comes from the tiles themselves, and a camera that knows where the level stops.

Godot 4.3+ 75–100 min 8 steps TileMapLayer

What you'll have at the end

  • A TileSet resource with a physics layer, so collision is a property of the tile rather than something you place by hand.
  • Terrain autotiling configured — you paint a blob of ground and Godot picks the corners and edges for you.
  • Three TileMapLayer nodes — background, ground, foreground — which is what the node was designed for.
  • A level with camera limits, walked end to end by a placeholder character.

You need a tileset PNG to start

Use your own if you have one. If not, grab a free pack — Kenney has CC0 tilesets that need no attribution, and using someone else's art is not cheating in a sketch about level building. What matters is that it's a uniform grid with no padding; 16 × 16 or 32 × 32 both work fine.

Step 1

Import settings, before anything else

8 min

New 2D project. Drop your tileset PNG into the project, then select it in the FileSystem dock and open the Import tab. This is the step people skip and then spend an hour debugging.

  • Filter: off. Or set the project-wide default: Project Settings → Rendering → Textures → Canvas Textures → Default Texture Filter → Nearest. Linear filtering turns pixel art into mush and, worse, bleeds neighbouring tiles into each other.
  • Mipmaps: off. They cause tile edges to sample from the wrong place when the camera zooms.
  • Click Reimport. It won't apply otherwise.

While you're in Project Settings, set the window size to something small and honest — 640 × 360 with Stretch Mode: canvas_items and Aspect: keep. Pixel art at a 1080p window is drawn so small you can't judge it.

The one-pixel seam

If you later see thin lines of the neighbouring tile along tile edges, it's almost always this step: filtering on, mipmaps on, or a fractional camera zoom. Fix it here rather than by adding padding to your texture.

Step 2

TileMapLayer and the TileSet resource

12 min

New 2D Scene, save it as level.tscn. Add a TileMapLayer node and rename it Ground.

In the inspector, on Tile Set, choose New TileSet. Click it to open it, and set the tile size to match your PNG — 16 × 16 or 32 × 32.

Now the bottom panel has TileSet and TileMap tabs. In TileSet, add an Atlas source with the +, and pick your PNG. Godot offers to auto-create tiles for every non-empty cell — say yes.

Switch to the TileMap tab and paint a few tiles into the viewport, just to confirm the pipeline works. Delete them afterwards — step 5 is where the level actually gets built.

Why one node per layer

Before Godot 4.3 a single TileMap node held every layer internally. Now each layer is its own node, which means you can hide one while you work, move it, give it its own modulate or z-index, or script it independently. It also means any tutorial showing one TileMap with a layers list inside it predates 4.3 — a quick way to date what you're watching.

Step 3

Collision, as a property of the tile

14 min

In the TileSet tab, select the TileSet resource itself and add a Physics Layer with +. Leave the collision layer and mask at their defaults for now.

Then select your atlas source, switch the tile editor to Select mode, and choose the tiles that should be solid. In the inspector for the selected tiles you'll now see Physics → Physics Layer 0, with a small polygon editor. There's a button to reset the polygon to a full rectangle — for square ground tiles that's all you need, and it's one click per tile.

Do the fill and all the edge tiles. Skip decorative tiles, background tiles, and anything the player should walk through.

Why this is worth the fifteen minutes

Collision now travels with the tile. Paint a platform anywhere in any level, and it's solid — no StaticBody2D, no CollisionShape2D, nothing to keep in sync when you move things around. Coming from hand-placed collision this feels like cheating the first time you see it work.

Select multiple tiles first

You can drag-select a whole run of tiles in the atlas and apply the collision polygon to all of them at once. Doing it one at a time for a 13-tile set is twenty minutes of clicking that a rectangle-drag turns into one.

Step 4

Terrain autotiling and peering bits

18 min

This is the fiddliest part of the sketch and the one that pays off most. Configured properly, you paint a rough blob and Godot picks the correct corner, edge and fill tiles as you go.

On the TileSet resource, add a Terrain Set, then add one terrain inside it and name it after your material. Set the terrain set's Mode to Corners and Sides — it's the most flexible and matches a 13-tile set.

Now the part that confuses everyone. Select your tiles, switch the tile editor to the Terrains section, and paint peering bits onto each tile. You are not saying "this is a corner tile". You're saying, for each of its edges and corners, what material is on that side.

Work through the set: the fill tile has everything marked; a top edge has everything except the top row; an outer top-left corner has everything except the top and left. Once done, switch to the TileMap tab, pick the Terrains tab in the tile palette, and paint with the terrain rather than an individual tile.

If it paints the wrong tiles, the bits are wrong — not the tileset

The usual symptom is a tile that looks right in isolation but produces gaps or wrong corners in a run. Go back and check that tile's peering bits against its neighbours' — the most common mistake is marking the four sides but forgetting the corners in Corners and Sides mode.

When to stop fighting it

Godot 4's terrain matching is less forgiving than Godot 3's autotiles were, and for irregular sets it can be genuinely stubborn. If you've checked the bits twice and it still misbehaves, Better Terrain is a well-regarded plugin that replaces this system with a more predictable one. Reaching for it isn't giving up — but do try the built-in one first, since it's what every tutorial and every collaborator will assume.

Step 5

Paint a level worth walking

14 min

Now the fun part, and the reason for all that setup. Paint with the terrain and build something roughly 60 tiles wide — wide enough that the camera has to move.

Include these deliberately, because each one tests a different part of what you just configured:

  • A long flat run, to check the fill and top edge
  • A staircase of single-tile steps, which is where corner bits fail if they're wrong
  • A floating platform, enclosed on all four sides
  • A pit with vertical walls, exercising the side edges
  • A narrow one-tile gap, to find out whether your set can even do it

The moment it clicks

Drag the terrain brush across an area and watch the edges resolve themselves behind the cursor. If the setup is right this is genuinely satisfying — and if any shape produces a wrong tile, you've found a peering-bit error in the most efficient way available.

Step 6

Depth — background and foreground layers

10 min

Add two more TileMapLayer nodes as siblings: Background above Ground in the tree, and Foreground below it. In Godot's 2D rendering, later siblings draw on top.

Assign the same TileSet resource to both — drag it from Ground's inspector, or pick it from the resource dropdown. One tileset, three layers.

  • Background: distant walls, cave interior, sky detail. Set its Modulate to a dimmer, slightly blue-shifted colour — the cheapest possible atmospheric perspective, and it does more than any amount of extra art.
  • Foreground: a few tiles in front of the player — grass tufts at the bottom of the screen, an overhanging branch. Use sparingly; it's seasoning.

Why the same TileSet

Reusing one resource across all three layers means editing a tile's collision or terrain once updates everywhere, and it keeps the level to a single texture. Three separate TileSets is the setup that seems tidier for about a day and then becomes three places to fix everything.

Step 7

Something to walk it with, and a camera that stops

12 min

Add a CharacterBody2D with a CollisionShape2D (a capsule, slightly narrower than one tile) and a Sprite2D using icon.svg scaled down. Add a Camera2D as a child of it.

Now the camera. Select the Camera2D and set:

  • Zoom to (3, 3) or (4, 4) — whole numbers only. A fractional zoom on pixel art produces shimmering edges as it moves.
  • Position Smoothing → Enabled, speed around 5. Instantly better, one checkbox.
  • Limit → left, top, right, bottom, in pixels. For a 60-tile-wide level of 16-pixel tiles, the right limit is 60 × 16 = 960. Set the others to match your level's extent.

Limits are in pixels, not tiles

Everyone types 60 the first time and wonders why the camera won't move. Multiply by your tile size. If you're unsure of the extent, click the last tile in the TileMap editor and read its coordinates in the status bar.

Step 8

Walk the whole thing

12 min

This is the sketch. A level looks finished in the editor and reveals itself in play, and fifteen minutes of walking teaches more than an hour of painting.

Run it and go end to end. Look for these specifically:

CheckWhat you're looking forUsual cause
SeamsThin lines between tiles as the camera movesTexture filtering on, or a fractional camera zoom
Invisible wallsGetting stuck where nothing is drawnCollision left on a decorative tile
Ghost floorsFalling through something solid-lookingCollision polygon missing on an edge tile
Camera driftLevel edge scrolling past into empty spaceLimits unset, or set in tiles instead of pixels
Wrong cornersA notch or mismatched tile on the staircasePeering bits on that tile

Then push it: set the camera zoom to 2 and walk again. A level built at 4× zoom often looks sparse when you can see more of it, and that's useful to know before you spend an evening decorating.

The one worth dwelling on

Turn the Background layer off and on while the game runs, from the Remote scene tree. The difference a dimmed duplicate of your own tiles makes is out of all proportion to the ten minutes it took, and it's the cheapest depth you will ever add to anything.

Write down the one thing that broke. Level-building bugs are extremely repeatable — whatever caught you here will catch you again, and knowing your own is worth more than the level.

That's the sketch

You've turned a grid of pixels into somewhere you can walk, and the useful part is knowing that collision and edge-matching are properties of the tileset rather than work you do per level. Set that up once and every level after this one is just painting. The level is disposable.
