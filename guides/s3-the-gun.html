The gun

Bullets that travel the way you face, stop at walls, damage what they hit, and clean themselves up. The player's only offensive verb, built as plainly as it can be — which turns out to be very plainly, because horizontal-only aiming removes most of the problem.

Godot 4.3+ 45–75 min 6 steps base game

What the game will have that it doesn't now

  • A bullet scene that flies, hits, and frees itself — with no leak when it misses.
  • Firing from the player's facing, with a fire-rate cooldown.
  • A collision layer scheme deciding what bullets hit, which the enemy and player assignments both plug into.
  • A take_damage() convention that anything shootable can implement.

What this deliberately leaves plain

No muzzle flash, no recoil, no screen kick, no tracer, no sound, no hitstop. A shot that connects should feel like a small event, and right now it will feel like nothing at all — that's the impact-feel assignment's whole job, and it needs something to add feel to first.

What you need

A player that moves and exposes a facing variable, and a fire action in the input map. If you don't have either, a CharacterBody2D that doesn't move at all is enough to build and test the gun — set var facing := 1 by hand and flip it with a key.

Step 1

Decide the collision layers, once

8 min

Do this before writing anything. Collision layers are the kind of decision that's five minutes now and an afternoon of confusion later, and every remaining assignment plugs into it.

Open Project Settings → Layer Names → 2D Physics and name five layers:

LayerNameWho is on itWho looks for it
1wallsArena boundary and solid tilesPlayer, enemies, bullets
2floorsOne-way platformsPlayer, enemies
3playerThe player bodyEnemies
4enemiesEvery walkerBullets, player
5bulletsPlayer bullets— nothing looks for these

Naming them means the inspector shows words rather than numbered checkboxes, which is the difference between reading a mask at a glance and counting squares.

Layer versus mask, in one sentence

An object's layer is what it is; its mask is what it looks for. A bullet is on bullets and masks walls and enemies — so it collides with those two and passes straight through everything else, including the floors and the player who fired it.

Step 2

The bullet scene

12 min
  1. New Scene → Other Node → Area2D, rename it Bullet.
  2. Add a CollisionShape2D with a small RectangleShape2D, about 6 × 2.
  3. Add a ColorRect the same size, offset so it's centred. Any bright colour.
  4. Add a VisibleOnScreenNotifier2D — this is how the bullet knows it has left the screen.
  5. On the Area2D, set Collision → Layer to bullets only, and Mask to walls and enemies only.
  6. Save as bullet.tscn.

Why Area2D rather than a physics body

A bullet doesn't want physics. It shouldn't be stopped, pushed, or slid along a surface — it wants to move in a straight line and be told when it overlaps something. That's exactly what an Area2D is for, and it avoids a whole category of bullets-bouncing-off-corners weirdness you'd get from a CharacterBody2D.

Step 3

Make it fly, and make it die

12 min

Attach a script to the Bullet root:

A bullet that never dies is a real bug

Miss everything, and without the screen-exited connection that bullet flies off forever, still processing every frame. A few minutes of held fire and you have thousands of them. The arena has walls on all sides so most bullets will hit one — but the notifier is the guarantee, and it costs one line.

Why has_method rather than checking a type

The bullet doesn't need to know what an enemy is. Anything that implements take_damage can be shot — a walker now, a barrel or a boss later — and nothing has to be rewritten to include them. It's the cheapest possible version of an interface, and in a project this size it's the right amount of ceremony.

Step 4

Firing

12 min

Back on the player. Add an export for the bullet scene and a cooldown:

Call _shoot(delta) from _physics_process, then select the Player in the arena and drag bullet.tscn into its Bullet Scene slot in the inspector.

The empty slot

Forget to assign the scene and firing does nothing at all, silently — no error, no bullet. If your first test produces no bullets, check that slot before you check the code.

Held fire, not tapped

is_action_pressed rather than just_pressed means holding the button fires continuously at the cooldown rate. In a game where enemies arrive in a stream, you want to hold; making the player mash would be a wrist tax rather than a skill.

Step 5

Something to shoot

10 min

You need a target before you can tell whether any of this works, and a throwaway one takes two minutes:

  1. New Scene → Other Node → StaticBody2D, named Target.
  2. A CollisionShape2D and a ColorRect, roughly enemy-sized.
  3. Set its Collision → Layer to enemies, and clear its mask — it doesn't need to look for anything.
  4. Attach this script, save the scene, and place three of them around the arena.

Why bother with a fake target

Because otherwise this assignment ends with "the code looks right", which is not the same as working. Two minutes of scaffolding turns an untested system into a tested one, and the take_damage signature you're testing against is the one the real enemy will implement.

Step 6

Shoot at things and find the numbers

12 min

This is the assignment. Run it and work through the checks, then push the two numbers that matter to their extremes.

CheckWhat a failure looks like
Bullets go the way you faceThey always fly right — facing isn't being read, or isn't updating
Bullets stop at wallsThey fly through — the mask is missing walls
Bullets pass through floorsThey vanish on the platform you're standing on — floors is in the mask and shouldn't be
Targets take two hitsOne hit kills — the damage amount or the target's health is off
Held fire is a steady streamA burst then nothing — cooldown isn't resetting
Remote tree stays cleanBullet nodes accumulating — something isn't freeing. This is the one you'd otherwise never notice

That last one is worth doing properly: run the game, switch the Scene dock to Remote, hold fire for ten seconds and watch the node list. It should stay roughly flat. If it grows without bound, you have a leak now that will become a frame-rate collapse in ten minutes of play.

Then push the two numbers

Set fire_interval to 0.05 and hold — a firehose, and the game becomes about nothing. Set it to 0.6 and every shot is a decision you have to be right about. The interesting range is somewhere between, and where exactly depends on how many enemies arrive per second, which you don't know yet. Note both extremes and leave it near 0.18.

Same with bullet SPEED: at 120 you lead your targets and it feels like a lobbed rock; at 900 it's effectively instant. Slower bullets make positioning matter more, which suits this game — worth remembering when the feel pass comes.

Delete the targets when you're done. Write down the fire interval and bullet speed you settled on, and how they felt — the impact-feel assignment starts from those numbers.

That's the assignment

The game now has its verb. It doesn't feel like anything yet — that's deliberate and it's next in line — but the mechanism underneath is complete, and the take_damage convention means everything you add later is shootable for free.
