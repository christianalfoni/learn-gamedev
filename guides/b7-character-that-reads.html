The character that reads

One character, drawn in the order that actually works: silhouette, flats, light, ramp, outline. No animation, no engine, no export. The whole sketch is about whether the thing is legible at the size it will really be seen.

Aseprite 75–100 min 8 steps 32 × 32

What you'll have at the end

  • One finished 32 × 32 character built in five deliberate passes rather than drawn all at once and fiddled with.
  • An eight-colour palette with hue-shifted ramps that you chose, and can explain.
  • A repeatable squint test for catching a shape that doesn't read, while it's still cheap to fix.
  • Three deliberately broken copies, so you know what each rule is actually buying you.

How this guide is built

Drawing is taught by video here, not by me — I can't show you a brush stroke in prose, and prep video 01 is a pixel artist doing this exact process on screen. What this guide adds is the order of operations, the constraints, and a way to check your own work when there's nobody to critique it.

Step 1

Decide everything you can before drawing

8 min

New file, 32 × 32. Then commit to four things and write them somewhere you can see:

  • Eight colours, maximum. You'll build them properly in step 4 — for now just accept the ceiling.
  • One light source, upper-left. Not "generally lit". A specific direction.
  • One character concept in five words. "Short angry blacksmith with hammer" is a design. "A guy" is not, and it'll show.
  • Facing three-quarter or side-on, whichever suits the concept. Front-on is the hardest to make readable and the most tempting to start with.

In Aseprite: turn on Pixel-Perfect in the pencil options, and set View → Grid → Grid Settings to 1 × 1 so you can toggle a pixel grid when you need it.

Why the light direction is a decision, not a detail

Every shading choice for the rest of the sketch is downstream of it. Pick it now and every later "should this be lighter?" has an answer you can check rather than feel. Change it halfway and you'll repaint everything — which is exactly the loop this sketch exists to break you out of.

Step 2

Silhouette only — one colour, no detail

15 min

Draw the character as a solid black shape. No face, no outline, no interior lines. Just the mass. Spend the full fifteen minutes here even if it feels like nothing is happening — this is where the character is actually decided.

Things that make a silhouette read at 32 pixels:

  • Distinct proportions. An oversized head, a wide stance, a hunched back. Realistic proportions turn to mush at this size.
  • Gaps. Space between an arm and the body is what tells you there is an arm. Two pixels of background beat ten of shading.
  • One strong outward feature. A hat, a hammer, a ponytail, a hunch — one thing that makes the shape unmistakable from across the room.

The squint test

Zoom to 100% and squint until the shape is blurry, or step back from the screen. Can you still tell what it is and which way it faces? If not, no amount of colour will fix it — go back and exaggerate the proportions. This test costs three seconds and you should run it after every step from here on.

Step 3

Build the palette before you use it

12 min

Open the palette panel, make a new palette, and build eight swatches deliberately: three ramps of two (skin, clothing, metal or hair), plus a dark for the outline and one saturated accent.

A ramp is a base colour and a shadow that belong together. Here's the rule that separates amateur pixel art from the rest, and it's the one thing to take from this whole sketch:

Why shadows shift hue

A shadow isn't the absence of light, it's a different light — sky bounce, usually cooler and bluer. Darkening by brightness alone produces the grey-brown mush that makes pixel art look cheap. Rotate the hue toward blue or purple on the way down and toward yellow on the way up, and the same eight colours suddenly look like a lit scene.

Lock it

Once the eight are in the palette, use only the palette. The moment you colour-pick something "close enough" from the colour wheel you have nine colours, then twelve, and the piece loses the coherence that made the constraint worth having.

Step 4

Flats — colour, still no shading

10 min

On a new layer above the silhouette, block in the base colour of each ramp — skin, shirt, trousers, metal. Flat fills only. Nothing lighter, nothing darker.

This is the step where the character stops being a shape and starts being a person, and it's also the last easy chance to fix a proportion. Do the squint test again: with colour added, does the head still read as a head?

Keep the silhouette layer

Don't paint over it — keep it underneath, hidden. In step 8 you'll want to toggle it back on to check the shape hasn't drifted while you were adding detail, and re-deriving it later is a waste of ten minutes.

Step 5

One light source, two tones

15 min

Now the shadow half of each ramp, and nothing else. Light comes from upper-left, so:

  • Every surface facing down and right takes the shadow colour.
  • Surfaces facing up and left stay on the base colour.
  • Under overhangs — the chin, the brim of a hat, an arm across the chest — take shadow regardless of which way they face. Contact shadow does more for readability than any other single choice.

Resist adding a third tone. Two tones per ramp forces you to decide where the form actually turns, which is the skill; three tones lets you blur that decision.

What to look for

The character should suddenly have volume — arms in front of a torso rather than glued onto it. If it looks noisy instead of solid, you've shaded detail rather than form: the shadow should follow the big shapes, not outline every small one.

Step 6

The outline decision

10 min

Three options, and this sketch is about knowing the difference rather than always picking the same one. Try all three on copies — Ctrl+D duplicates the sprite in a new frame, which makes comparison instant.

  • Solid dark outline. Maximum readability against any background. Reads as cartoon, slightly stickerish, and eats two of your eight colours' worth of visual weight.
  • Selective outline. Outline only where the character meets the background, dropped or lightened where light hits the top edge. Most modern pixel art does this.
  • No outline. Shapes defined by colour alone. Elegant, and fragile — it fails the moment the background is a similar value.

Outlines are a background decision

Which one is right depends entirely on what the character will sit on. A sprite that will move across grass, stone and sky needs a dark outline to survive all three. A sprite on a known dark background can go without and look far better for it. There's no correct answer in the abstract, which is why picking one deliberately is the exercise.

Step 7

Details, and the one that earns its place

10 min

Eyes, a belt buckle, a highlight on the hammer. At 32 × 32 you get perhaps three of these before the sprite turns to noise, so choose them.

Use the accent colour from step 3 exactly once, on the thing you most want the eye to land on. A single saturated pixel in an otherwise muted piece is the cheapest focal point available.

Eyes at this scale are two pixels

Usually one dark pixel each, sometimes a single wide one. Anything more and you've spent your detail budget on a face nobody sees at actual size. If the character needs an expression, put it in the silhouette — a tilted head says more than a pixel eyebrow.

Step 8

Break it three ways

15 min

This is the sketch. Everything above was so this step has something to work on.

Duplicate the finished sprite into three more frames and deliberately ruin each one. You're not looking for which is better — you already know. You're building the ability to see what each rule was doing:

Break itHowWhat you should see
Kill the hue shiftReplace each shadow with the base colour darkened by brightness onlyFlat, muddy, lifeless — the same drawing with the air removed
Move the lightShade as if lit from the lower-right insteadUncanny and slightly sinister; up-lighting reads as horror for a reason
Shade the detailAdd a third and fourth tone everywhere you canNoise. More information, less readable — the core trade of pixel art

Then the final check: put all four on backgrounds of very different values — near-black, mid-grey, near-white — and view at 100%. Set the canvas background colour in Aseprite or drop a filled layer underneath. The one that survives all three is the one that would survive a real game.

The one that surprises people

The mid-grey background. Sprites are usually drawn on dark or transparent canvases, and plenty of them fall apart on mid-grey because their values sit too close to it. If yours vanishes, the fix is contrast in the silhouette, not more shading.

Write down which break bothered you most. That's the rule you'd been applying by habit rather than by choice, and it's the one worth watching next time.

That's the sketch

You've drawn one character, and more usefully you've drawn it in an order that makes each decision checkable instead of a matter of taste. The squint test and the hue-shifted ramp are the two things worth keeping; the character is disposable.
